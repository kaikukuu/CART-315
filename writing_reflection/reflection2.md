Reflection 2: Reflective Conversation with Materials
2026-09/29

I constantly think about design and why/how designs are conceived. I design in particular for myself and my own needs BUT I don't think all my designs are only useful to ME. I really appreciate the ease of use when I find something works because I want to live with as little frustration as I can when possible. I even document this for myself in case I forget what I enjoyed or when there is a specific use-case that it fails. Often my design process begins with something I already like that has some kind of ease of use problem. For instance when designing clothes and altering sewing/knitted patterns I will reference and adapt designs from clothes I enjoy wearing. A new design will be conceived when something has qualities I really like but there's some part of it that makes using it uncomfortable or frustrating to use. As a concrete example: I recently thrifted a shirt that had all the qualities I was looking for. It had interesting texture and appearance, cotton fibre, a colour that complements the rest of my closet, comfortable but... it didn't sit right on me and would shift awkwardly so I had a hard time styling it into the silhouette I wanted. This led me to experimenting with adding adjustable straps that allow the shape of the shirt to stay in place while not sacrificing the original shirt's design (I cut no fabric from the pattern). 

I'd never been able to put it into word so it struck me when Shön discussed how designs can be thought of as systems (Such as representations of how the user interacts and how the components of the design work together to produce some type of result). In a reflective designing process questions beget questions meaning the design process can produce "compounding" problems or unanticipated consequences (or benefits) which help a designer to gain a better understanding of the "problem space". In the section on judgement I really appreciate the idea of backtalk. The backtalk cycle revealing unintended uses for an artifact is very fascinating. Consumers can "project a different meaning from that intended by the original designer," thus setting in motion revolutionizing artifacts/tools such as the example of Scotch tape despite being unintentional. There's a design that may already exist waiting to be used in a new way. One way you could do this is by purposefully ignoring the intention of the designer and using reflective techniques identify new uses outside of its context. Sometimes in desperation you can try something you would have never considered previously and discover a better way to use or implement it.  There could be totally different fields that a design has never being applied in just because no one really considered its use outside of its intention. I also found the "gyroscope for taste" to be another interesting topic as I agree it is a skill to have good taste, to be able to tell when you're onto something and the ability to judge your own work. At the same time it's difficult to quantify what taste even is since it is both objective and subjective at the same time along with multidimetional and extremely personal.
